## 1. Introduction

Random walks on cycles are the simplest non-trivial example where hitting and cover times can be computed exactly. The results here are classical in spirit. Our purpose is to present compact proofs that an AI agent found, together with the human explanation needed to make them teachable.

Throughout, $X_t$ is the lazy walk on $\mathbb{Z}_n$: at each step it stays put with probability $1/2$, and otherwise moves to $X_t \pm 1$ with probability $1/4$ each.

## 2. Hitting times

**Theorem 1.** Let $\tau_k$ be the first time the lazy walk started at $0$ hits $k \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}$. Then $\mathbb{E}[\tau_k] = 2k(n-k)$.

*Proof.* Unwrap the cycle and track the walk on $\mathbb{Z}$, started at $0$. Hitting $k$ on the cycle is the same as the unwrapped walk exiting the interval $(k - n, k)$. Write $S_t$ for the unwrapped position. Each increment has mean $0$ and variance $1/2$, so $M_t = S_t^2 - t/2$ is a martingale. The exit time $\tau$ of $(k-n, k)$ has finite expectation, so optional stopping applies:
$$\mathbb{E}[S_\tau^2] = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\tau].$$
By gambler's ruin, $S_\tau = k$ with probability $\frac{n-k}{n}$ and $S_\tau = k-n$ with probability $\frac{k}{n}$. Therefore
$$\mathbb{E}[S_\tau^2] = k^2 \cdot \tfrac{n-k}{n} + (n-k)^2 \cdot \tfrac{k}{n} = k(n-k),$$
and $\mathbb{E}[\tau] = 2k(n-k)$. $\blacksquare$

*Intuition (added by the authors).* A non-lazy walk needs about $k(n-k)$ steps, which is the familiar "distance to each end, multiplied" from gambler's ruin. Laziness wastes half the steps, so the answer doubles.

## 3. Cover time

**Theorem 2.** The expected cover time of the lazy walk on $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is $n(n-1)$.

*Proof.* The set of visited vertices is always an arc. When the arc has $j$ vertices, the walk sits at one of its endpoints. To visit a new vertex it must exit the arc, which on the unwrapped line means exiting an interval with $j$ interior points, starting next to one end. By the computation in Theorem 1 this takes $2 \cdot 1 \cdot j = 2j$ expected steps. Summing over $j = 1, \dots, n-1$:
$$\mathbb{E}[\text{cover}] = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} 2j = n(n-1). \qquad \blacksquare$$
