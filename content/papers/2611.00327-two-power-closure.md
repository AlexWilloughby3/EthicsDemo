## 1. Introduction

The paper arXiv:2611.00142 showed that every solution $n > 1$ of $n \mid 2^n + 1$ is divisible by $3$, and listed the 18 solutions up to $20{,}000$. Looking at that list, solutions seem to come in chains: $171, 513, 1539, 4617, 13851$ are each three times the last. We prove that this always happens.

Both results we use from arXiv:2611.00142 have proofs that named people understand, and that paper is Level 1. So this paper inherits nothing opaque. Building on fully understood work keeps a paper at Level 1.

## 2. Closure under multiplication by 3

**Theorem 1.** If $n \mid 2^n + 1$, then $3n \mid 2^{3n} + 1$.

*Proof.* Factor
$$2^{3n} + 1 = (2^n + 1)(2^{2n} - 2^n + 1).$$
By hypothesis $n$ divides the first factor. Since $2^n + 1$ is odd, $n$ is odd, so $2^n \equiv (-1)^n = -1 \pmod 3$. Hence
$$2^{2n} - 2^n + 1 \equiv 1 + 1 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod 3.$$
So $n$ divides the first factor and $3$ divides the second, which gives $3n \mid 2^{3n} + 1$. $\blacksquare$

## 3. An infinite family

**Corollary 2.** For every $a \ge 2$, $3^a \cdot 19$ is a solution.

*Proof.* $171 = 3^2 \cdot 19$ appears in the exhaustive search of arXiv:2611.00142. Apply Theorem 1 repeatedly. $\blacksquare$

This gives infinitely many solutions that are not powers of $3$. The first few are $171, 513, 1539, 4617, 13851, 41553, \dots$

## 4. Primitive solutions

Call a solution $m$ **primitive** if $m = 1$, or if $m/3$ is not a solution (including when $3 \nmid m$).

**Proposition 3.** Every solution is $3^k m$ for some $k \ge 0$ and some primitive solution $m$. Every primitive solution $m > 1$ is divisible by $3$. The primitive solutions up to $20{,}000$ are exactly $1, 171, 3249, 13203$.

*Proof.* Starting from a solution $n$, divide by $3$ as long as the result is still a solution. This stops at a primitive solution $m$, with $n = 3^k m$. If $m > 1$, then $3 \mid m$ by Theorem 4 of arXiv:2611.00142. For the list, check each of the 18 solutions from that paper's exhaustive search. $\blacksquare$

By Theorem 1, every primitive solution $m$ generates the infinite chain $m, 3m, 9m, \dots$. Since $3 \cdot 1 = 3$, the chain from $m = 1$ is exactly the powers of $3$ from arXiv:2611.00142's Proposition 3. Whether there are infinitely many primitive solutions is left open.
