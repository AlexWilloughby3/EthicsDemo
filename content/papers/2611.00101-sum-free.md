## 1. Introduction

Questions about additive structure in sets of integers go back to Schur, who showed in 1916 that the positive integers cannot be finitely colored without some color class containing a solution of $x + y = z$. A natural "dual" question is how large a sum-free subset one can always find inside an arbitrary finite set.

This note works through the standard answer. Our aim is pedagogical: every step is written out, and no result beyond basic modular arithmetic and linearity of expectation is used.

## 2. Statement of the theorem

**Theorem 1.** Let $B = \{b_1, \dots, b_n\}$ be a set of nonzero integers. Then $B$ contains a sum-free subset $A$ with $|A| > n/3$.

The key idea is that a sum-free set is easy to find in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$: the "middle third" of the residues. We then use a random dilation to transport $B$ into that middle third.

## 3. Proof

Choose a prime $p = 3k + 2$ with $p > 2\max_i |b_i|$. Such primes exist by Dirichlet's theorem. Let
$$C = \{k+1, k+2, \dots, 2k+1\} \subset \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}.$$

*$C$ is sum-free mod $p$.* If $x, y \in C$ then $2k+2 \le x + y \le 4k+2$. Reducing mod $p = 3k+2$, the sum lands either in $[2k+2, 3k+1]$ or in $[0, k]$, and neither range meets $C$.

*$C$ is large.* $|C| = k + 1$, and $\frac{k+1}{p-1} = \frac{k+1}{3k+1} > \frac{1}{3}$.

Now pick $x$ uniformly at random from $\{1, \dots, p-1\}$. Since $p \nmid b_i$, the map $x \mapsto x b_i \bmod p$ is a bijection on the nonzero residues, so $x b_i \bmod p$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,p-1\}$. Hence
$$\Pr[x b_i \bmod p \in C] = \frac{|C|}{p-1} > \frac{1}{3},$$
and by linearity of expectation the expected number of $i$ with $x b_i \bmod p \in C$ is greater than $n/3$. Fix an $x$ achieving at least this expectation and let $A = \{ b_i : x b_i \bmod p \in C\}$.

If $a_1 + a_2 = a_3$ with all $a_j \in A$, then $x a_1 + x a_2 \equiv x a_3 \pmod p$, contradicting that $C$ is sum-free. So $A$ is sum-free and $|A| > n/3$. $\blacksquare$

## 4. Remarks

The constant $1/3$ cannot be replaced by anything larger than $1/3$ in general. Determining the best lower-order term is a much harder problem that has seen progress in the last decade. We leave the details to the references in our seminar notes.

**Arithmetic progressions.** A natural companion question replaces $x + y = z$ with $k$-term arithmetic progressions. Here much less is understood by hand. Using the machine-verified value $W(2,5) = 178$ [arXiv:2611.00301]:

**Corollary 4.2.** Every 2-coloring of $\{1, \dots, 178\}$ has a color class containing a 5-term arithmetic progression.

This is immediate from the definition of $W(2,5)$. We include it because it shows how an otherwise fully human-understood note can come to depend on a result whose proof no human has digested. Under the proposed guidelines, that single citation moves this note from Level 1 to Level 2.
